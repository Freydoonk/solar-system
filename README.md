# Solar System

A React / TypeScript solar-system explorer with an interactive Three.js 3D view, a shared simulation clock, date-based planetary positions, Moon phases and astronomical seasons. A 2D overview remains available.

The separate **Observe** page plans telescope observations from a geographic location. Both sections are installable as a PWA, with offline calculations and bundled surface maps.

For development and AI-assisted changes, start with [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md): architecture and data flow, feature-to-file/test map, behaviour that must be preserved, and validation commands. This README describes how to use and deploy the app.

## Interactive lessons

Open **Learn** for guided lessons on Mars's retrograde motion, Venus's phases and why full Moon does not always mean a lunar eclipse. Each has four checkpoints, previous/next controls, a shared UTC time slider and optional playback. A space diagram and an Earth-view plot/normalized phase disc update together. The lesson clock does not alter Explore or Observe. Position markers are annotations; orbital distance diagrams use one scale, while the Mars coordinate plot and normalized phase drawings explicitly state their conventions. Lunar shadow geometry is approximate and the phase disc does not render eclipse darkening. Playback pauses work while hidden and stops advancing at the end. Lessons load lazily and are available offline. Small portrait screens stack the views; landscape/desktop displays put them side by side.

## Telescope planner

Observe uses one tool navigation row: **Night plan**, **Night chart**, **Events**, **Star atlas**, **Pointing**, **Eyepiece**, **Observing times**, **Find by direction**, **Notebook** and **My horizon**. Each tool opens directly, without a second row of telescope tabs. The location/time summary stays above the tools; **Location & time** opens a separate settings drawer. Telescope tools share one target selector and retain their settings when switching. Hidden renderers/searches stop work. The tool navigation scrolls horizontally on narrow screens. Night-plan and event actions open the related eyepiece directly.

**Sky** is a separate main tab (`#sky`) for a live, observer-centered 3D sky. Hold your phone with its screen toward you and move it left/right or up/down to find the Sun, Moon, planets, stars and conventional constellation patterns. It uses the bundled star/Messier catalogue and local astronomy calculations. The Sun has a gold disc and halo; **Show Sun** centers it in the simulated view and switches to manual control. When it is below the horizon or hidden by **My horizon**, a status explains why it is absent. Labels identify objects near the center; controls toggle constellation lines, labels and Messier markers. The ground includes your personal horizon. Marker sizes are annotations, not a magnified telescope image or a prediction of visibility in daylight. The Sun marker is separate from Observe's night-time telescope targets.

In **Location & controls**, use **Use device location** or enter coordinates; an IP estimate is clearly labelled until you replace it. Location and the horizon mask are shared with Observe in tab memory. Sky always uses the current time, independently of the Explore and Observe clocks, and updates sky positions once per minute. Device/manual location and orientation readings are not stored or transmitted to another service. The simulated scene does not use the camera.

Sky uses a dark map by default, showing objects at their current positions even in daylight. Enable **Match daylight** in **Location & controls** to make the background follow the current Sun altitude and let stars, planets, constellation patterns and Messier markers fade through twilight and disappear in daylight. The Sun and Moon retain their position markers above the horizon. Both modes use the same live positions. This is a clear-sky illustration, not a simulation of clouds, weather, light pollution or exact naked-eye visibility.

Select **Enable phone motion** to request sensor permission. Motion requires HTTPS (or localhost) and a supported mobile browser. Earth-referenced orientation is used when available; relative-only browsers, including Safari configurations, require **Align to north** while the back of the upright phone points toward a known true-north landmark. Compass estimates can be inaccurate near metal or magnets, and magnetic north can differ from true north. Re-align if the sky drifts. Both portrait and landscape are supported. If permission is denied, sensors are missing, or you prefer manual control, drag the sky or use the arrow keys; + and − zoom. Dragging releases motion control. Leaving Sky or hiding the browser stops sensors/rendering; enable motion again on return. If WebGL fails, the direction readout remains and **Observe → Star atlas** provides a 2D alternative. Device permissions and physical compass accuracy depend on the browser and hardware.

**My horizon** accepts obstacle altitudes at eight true-north compass bearings, interpolating between them. The mask constrains night plans and observing windows, labels obstructed targets and shades the Star atlas. The original minimum 20° target / maximum −6° Sun thresholds remain. Obstruction edges are sampled every 30 seconds then refined; very narrow or grazing gaps can be missed. Leave a margin above uncertain estimates. The mask stays in tab memory and resets when the site changes; no coordinates or mask are saved in the notebook.

**Field mode** opens a full-screen red display with large altitude/azimuth readings, previous/next target controls and adjustable dimming. **Take this plan outside** carries a night plan's targets into the field display in visit order. Both actions start with live pointing. **Preview visit time** explicitly switches to the scheduled instant, with a prominent planned-time notice; **Now · live** returns to current conditions. The personal horizon is included in target status. Exit returns to the same planner, with its state intact. Lower device brightness separately: browser controls, notifications and system dialogs keep their normal colors. All calculations work offline, and no field session or location is stored persistently.

**Notebook** saves observations (target, selected instant, seen/not-seen/planned result, a 1–5 experience rating, equipment name and notes), a target list and named equipment setups on this device. Save/load setups under **What will I see? → My saved setups**. Native aperture, focal length and accessory values are validated. Edit/remove entries, filter notes, or undo the last notebook change. JSON export/import works offline; import merges IDs while retaining existing records. No coordinates are added automatically or exported as metadata (free-text notes contain whatever you write). Browser storage errors are shown; the current in-tab notebook remains exportable. Keep backups because browser data can be cleared. Limits: 1,000 entries, 100 setups, 100 target names and a 2 MB import file. Location and live planner state remain temporary.

**Star atlas** is a lazy, offline compass chart with 5,070 stars through visual magnitude 6, conventional lines for 88 constellations (Serpens in two parts) and all 110 Messier entries. Search by star name/HIP ID, Messier/common name, or constellation name; adjust the magnitude limit, toggle patterns/objects and zoom around the selected object. Selected targets show geometric altitude, true-north azimuth, equator-of-date RA/Dec and three nearby bright reference stars with angular separations. Messier targets are finding-chart entries, not textured eyepiece simulations. Map positions update once per minute while visible. HYG space motion and precession/nutation are included; stellar annual aberration/parallax and refraction are omitted. Conventional lines and marker sizes are annotations. [Catalogue sources and licences](public/catalogue/ATTRIBUTION.md) document the HYG CC BY-SA 4.0 subset and D3-Celestial BSD data.

**Events** searches 30/90/365 days for major Moon phases, outer-planet oppositions, Mercury/Venus greatest elongations and lunar eclipses. Each item explains the geometry and, when a site is chosen, reports altitude and Sun altitude at the exact instant. Eclipse contacts delimit the penumbral phase; the local status is at peak only and the 3D preview does not simulate the eclipse. Export one event or the filtered list as `.ics` to set reminders in your own calendar. Files use UTC instants and contain no location. Event search is local, cancellable and available offline.

The **Night chart** planning tab compares selectable Moon/planet altitude curves with daylight, twilight and astronomical darkness. Generate a fixed 24-hour plot, then drag/tap it or use its keyboard-accessible time slider to update Observe and its eyepiece. Curves use geometric altitude and approximately 15-minute samples; the numerical readings are recalculated at the selected instant. It does not change Explore's clock.

**What can I see tonight?** builds a 30, 60, 120 or 240 minute session within the next 24 hours from the selected instant. Ten-minute visits include time to move equipment. Unaided viewing excludes Uranus and Neptune; optical mode includes them without promising visibility through every instrument. Plans prefer more distinct targets, then higher altitude, while retaining the existing 20° target / −6° Sun thresholds. Each visit shows its direction, altitude, full available window and a button to load the target/time. Searches run in a cancellable worker and work offline; results keep their original search time. Sky conditions and obstructions still matter.

Open **Observe** in the top bar. Choose a location using the shared approximate GeoJS lookup, the explicit **Use device location** button, or manual latitude/longitude/elevation. Browser geolocation requests permission only after the button is pressed; denied or unavailable location leaves manual entry available. Device accuracy is displayed. Device/manual coordinates remain in tab memory and are not sent to an external API or saved in storage. A late IP/device result cannot replace a subsequently chosen manual site.

The planner has an independent clock, initially live. Times use either the device's named timezone (including daylight saving) or UTC; changing coordinates does not silently change timezone. The UTC instant is also displayed. Skipped local times and invalid calendar dates are rejected; repeated fall-back times use the browser's first occurrence, so choose UTC to specify the second occurrence unambiguously.

Choose the Moon or a planet from Mercury to Neptune, excluding Earth. Pointing uses observer-relative equatorial coordinates with light travel time and aberration, converted from equator-of-date to apparent altitude/azimuth with standard atmospheric refraction. Azimuth is clockwise from **true north**, not magnetic north. Alt-azimuth instructions and equatorial/GoTo instructions are both available, with a choice of equator-of-date or J2000 RA/Dec. Match the mount's coordinate epoch and complete its own alignment; this is a pointing guide, not hardware control.

**What will I see?** renders the selected target as a textured, sunlit 3D model. **In the eyepiece** fixes the view to the observer's direction, with the disc diameter scaled to its fraction of the telescope's approximate true field; tiny targets are not enlarged. The initial equipment values are explicitly an example. Enter telescope/eyepiece focal lengths, aperture, apparent field and optional Barlow/reducer factor to calculate magnification, field, exit pupil and aperture-related limitations. Changing date or site recalculates angular size, phase and the projected pole/ring opening. Below-horizon or daytime targets are labelled as planning previews. Image orientation offers north-up/east-left, 180° rotation and left/right mirroring.

**Explore in 3D** enlarges the model for inspection and clearly labels the different scale/viewpoint. Drag with a mouse or one finger to orbit; focused arrow keys rotate in small steps. **Reset to Earth view** restores the observer's angle. Returning to the eyepiece restores its scale and angle automatically. This renderer loads lazily, reuses the bundled textures, draws only on changes/interactions and stops drawing when Observe is hidden. WebGL or texture failures leave the numeric guide available.

The **Telescope model** catalogue groups commercial amateur instruments (Celestron PowerSeeker 70AZ, Sky-Watcher Heritage 130, Celestron NexStar 8SE), ground research facilities (Palomar's Hale, one ESO VLT Unit Telescope, one Keck telescope and GTC) and space observatories (Hubble and James Webb). Each profile shows its type, organization/site, aperture, wavelength range or observing mode, capabilities and an official source. Commercial presets apply published aperture/focal length with an explicitly illustrative, editable eyepiece/50° apparent field/no-Barlow setup. Editing native aperture or focal length returns to Custom; changing accessories retains the model selection.

Research profiles explain imaging/spectroscopy instead of inventing an eyepiece view or exposure forecast. Their 3D model is only a reference illustration. They do not relocate the planner to the observatory or compute spacecraft visibility. Known target exclusions are explicit (Webb: Moon/Mercury/Venus; Hubble: Mercury) and suppress the image preview. Other targets are not guaranteed schedulable. Profiles and source links are bundled for offline reading; opening external references needs connectivity. Catalogue specifications were checked on 26 October 2026; no prices, purchase recommendations or live instrument availability are implied.

The accompanying feature guide describes what to look for and the limits of ordinary visual observing. Static texture details, weather, brightness, visual resolution, eclipses and neighbouring moons/stars are not predicted. Surface prime-meridian alignment remains illustrative. The unit sphere uses mean radius and Saturn's approximate ring envelope is 2.4 radii; oblateness and ring shadows are not modelled. Optical formulas follow [Celestron telescope basics](https://celestron-site-support-files.s3.amazonaws.com/support_files/SkyPortal%20130-90-70%205%20language%20manual.pdf); general observing guidance follows [NASA skywatching](https://science.nasa.gov/skywatching/). Preview tests cover magnification/field relationships, invalid equipment inputs, actual 3D projection at tiny/clipped scales, observer-relative sizes, lunar illumination and Saturn's ring orientation across dates.

**Find observing times** searches the next 7, 30 or 365 days from the selected instant and returns up to five chronological windows. Windows require geometric target-center altitude ≥20° and geometric Sun altitude ≤−6°. Event searches find threshold crossings; culmination and interval boundaries select the highest altitude within each window. Polar daylight/night and circumpolar objects are handled without fabricating rise/set times. A separate worker keeps the interface responsive and can be cancelled. Results retain their explicit search start time; changing the planner clock does not silently change an existing result.

The compass map and direction lookup cover the listed planets and Moon only, not stars or deep-sky objects. A 10° matching cone is a direction aid, not the telescope's field of view. Clear skies, unobstructed horizons, seeing and equipment alignment are not predicted. The guide is for night-time use and excludes solar pointing; never aim a telescope or finder at the Sun.

Astronomy API reference: [Astronomy Engine](https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy/blob/master/source/js/README.md).

## Install and offline use

**Install app** offers the browser's install prompt when available, otherwise platform instructions. On iOS use Safari → Share → Add to Home Screen. The relative manifest, icon paths, worker scope and precache URLs support the GitHub Pages subpath.

The production build's postbuild script generates a content-versioned service worker and precaches all build assets, including the lazy pages, offline atlas, calculation workers and textures. Wait for **Offline ready** before leaving the network. Device storage may be evicted by the browser. No location responses or third-party resources are cached. Offline use supports manual coordinates and device geolocation when the device can provide it.

New versions wait until the app is reopened or **Update** is pressed; updating reloads and resets temporary tab state. Development mode does not register a service worker. Test PWA behavior with **npm run build** followed by **npm run preview**, using HTTPS or localhost. The installable icons are bundled; **scripts/generate-icons.ps1** regenerates them on Windows. [MDN installability](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps/Guides/Making_PWAs_installable) describes platform differences.

## Run

Use Node.js 25, matching the project's Docker images and deployment workflow.

```sh
npm ci
npm run dev
npm test
npm run build
```

The application is served under `/solar-system/`. `npm run lint` also checks the source; the unrelated `Ball.tsx` component currently has one existing hook dependency warning.

### Docker development

```sh
docker compose up -d --build
```

Open `http://localhost:5173/solar-system/`. The development service runs `npm ci` before Vite starts so its separate `/app/node_modules` volume matches the mounted `package-lock.json`. After adding or updating a dependency while the container is running, run `docker compose restart web`. Installing packages on the host alone does not update this volume. If dependency installation fails, inspect `docker compose logs web`; Vite starts only after installation succeeds.

`.dockerignore` keeps the host's `node_modules` out of Linux images. Production images install locked dependencies during the build; rebuild them after dependency changes.

## GitHub Pages

This repository already includes [the deployment workflow](.github/workflows/deploy.yml). Vite's `base` is `/solar-system/`, matching the repository name.

1. Open [Settings → Pages](https://github.com/Freydoonk/solar-system/settings/pages).
2. Under **Build and deployment → Source**, select **GitHub Actions**.
3. Commit your changes and push the `main` branch. The **Deploy to GitHub Pages** workflow installs the locked dependencies, runs tests, builds `dist`, and publishes it. You can also start it from **Actions → Deploy to GitHub Pages → Run workflow** after the desired commit is on GitHub.
4. When deployment succeeds, the site is available at **https://freydoonk.github.io/solar-system/**. The workflow's deployment link is the authoritative URL.

No Docker server or backend is required on Pages. Images, fonts, JavaScript chunks and textures are included in the build. Do not commit `node_modules` or `dist`. For a fork with a different repository name, change `base` in `vite.config.ts` to `/<repository-name>/`; for a root `username.github.io` site or a custom domain, use `/`.

To check the production build locally: `npm run build`, then `npm run preview`, and open `http://localhost:4173/solar-system/`.

References: [Vite's Pages guide](https://vite.dev/guide/static-deploy.html#github-pages), [GitHub's publishing-source instructions](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/configuring-a-publishing-source-for-your-github-pages-site).

## Controls

- **Help**, above the sky, opens the in-app guide for camera movement, touch, time travel and display conventions. Press **Esc** or **Close** to dismiss it.
- **3D explorer** opens centered on Earth, with its diameter occupying 15% of the sky viewport's shorter side. Only the camera distance changes; physical size and distance ratios remain intact. Switching directly to 2D uses the same initial framing. **Fit system** shows the whole solar system; **Recenter** or selecting a body gives a closer view.
- **Follow body** selects any planet, moon or the Sun. **Sun in view** locks the camera angle to keep the selected body and the Sun in the frame, mainly showing the body's night side. If a moon's parent blocks either sight line, the camera moves farther away; all physical geometry stays unchanged. A locator marks the Sun when it is smaller than a few pixels. **Free angle** follows the body's orbital motion while allowing manual viewing angles.
- Moons also offer **From [planet]** and **Behind moon · [planet] in view**. The first starts just outside the parent's physical radius/ring envelope on the moon-facing side and looks at the moon. Wheel zoom approaches the moon along that line, with a limit that prevents backing into the planet or rings; **Recenter** returns to the planet. This viewpoint follows the planet–moon direction, not a fixed surface location. The second looks from behind the moon toward its parent and fits the planet's entire disc/ring envelope in portrait or landscape. Both follow orbital motion and date changes at true scale; tiny moons retain a locator. Manual camera movement releases these angle locks too.
- Earth also has **My region**. On page load, a single HTTPS lookup through [GeoJS](https://www.geojs.io/docs/v1/endpoints/geo/) estimates the visitor's region from their public IP. The camera faces that region and follows Earth's terrestrial rotation and orbital movement, including date jumps. Wheel zoom keeps either angle lock. Dragging, touch gestures, focused arrow keys, **Release angle lock**, Top / Side or switching to 2D releases it. Choose a follow mode again to re-enable it. A late location response never overrides a manually adjusted view.
- Location is approximate; a VPN or mobile network can identify a different region. The lookup requests no GPS access and sends no cookies or referrer. Only rounded coordinates and a city/country label are held in memory; this app does not store the IP or location. GeoJS receives the normal request IP. Offline, blocked, invalid or timed-out lookups leave the normal Earth view available after a maximum four-second lookup wait; rendering starts immediately. The location service is the only external runtime request; textures and other assets remain bundled.
- Left-drag to orbit; hold **Space before left-dragging** to move left/right/up/down in the screen plane. Release Space to return to the chosen mode on the next drag. Select **Pan** under **Drag action**, or use right-drag. Select **Orbit** to rotate again. Scroll to approach/retreat. Touch: one finger follows the chosen Orbit/Pan mode, two fingers zoom/pan. Click or Tab into the sky to pan with arrow keys. Space shortcuts apply only while the sky is focused or hovered with no control focused, so fields and buttons retain their normal behaviour. Browser Ctrl/Cmd wheel shortcuts remain available.
- Click a body or its label, or choose it under **Follow body**, to fly to it and follow its movement. This includes every moon, even bodies too small to select in the overview. Camera framing follows date jumps too. **Recenter** restores the selected follow mode; **Top**, **Side**, and **Fit system** restore useful camera positions. Selecting a moon enables moon visibility; hiding moons while following one returns to its parent.
- On phones and small tablets the controls start collapsed, giving almost the whole screen to the sky. **Camera**, **Time**, and **Moon & seasons** open a scrollable panel; **Hide** collapses it again. Short landscape screens put the open panel beside the sky. Controls have larger touch targets, and the layout accounts for display cutouts and the home indicator. The compact following badge leaves the center of the sky clear.
- Camera movement uses slower orbit/pan/zoom with no inertia after release. **Fine control** lowers sensitivity further. Left / Right / Up / Down buttons rotate by 2°, or 0.25° in Fine control, and release any automatic angle lock. The cursor becomes a closed hand while dragging and resets after release/cancellation. Dragging away and back no longer selects a body as an accidental click.
- In the whole-system view, wheel zoom moves toward the pointer. When following a body, zoom stays centered on that body so you can examine its surface. The 3D camera can approach far more closely than the 2D zoom slider. Reduced-motion preferences disable camera flights.
- The Gregorian calendar and UTC time input select a moment between 1900 and 2100. Editing either pauses the simulation. **Play** continues from that moment, **Reset** returns to the last selected moment, and **Now** selects the current time.
- **Time speed** defaults to **Real time**, one simulated second per real second. All planets, moons, the calendar, Moon phase and seasons use the same timestamp. Background tabs do not accumulate a time jump.
- **Both views always use true sizes and distances.** One scene unit equals 1,000,000 km. The Sun, every planet, every moon, heliocentric distances and parent-relative satellite distances all use this exact same conversion. There are no independent size/distance controls, enlarged-body modes, orbit bands or Sun-size toggles.
- The Sun is always a physical sphere/disc at the same scale as the other bodies. Actual orbital separations keep the planets and moons outside it. Hollow locator rings and labels are annotations only; they never enlarge a body's surface.
- At true scale, most bodies are smaller than a pixel in the whole-system view. **Follow body** and **Recenter** work in both views and bring even tiny moons into view. **Fit system** restores the overview. Perspective in 3D and foreshortening in the top-down 2D projection affect apparent sizes/separations as expected.
- In **2D overview**, the single **Zoom** control multiplies both radii and distances together. Scroll toward the pointer or use the logarithmic slider, which spans 0.5×–10 billion× to reach small moons from the full solar system. Drag or use arrow keys to pan. A selected body stays centered as the simulation runs. Scrolling over the controls panel keeps its normal behaviour.
- The lower-right Earth panel shows the illuminated lunar disc and fraction, and the opposite astronomical seasons in both hemispheres. Its northern/southern view selector rotates the Moon illustration; orientation in a local observer's sky also depends on location and time.

## Astronomy model

[Astronomy Engine](https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy/tree/master/source/js) calculates heliocentric planet positions (including Pluto), Earth's Moon, and the four Galilean moons. Both views use the same physical scene: AU coordinates are converted to kilometres, then divided by the common scale. The 3D view retains all three J2000 ecliptic coordinates; 2D is their orthographic projection from ecliptic north. No logarithmic or per-orbit remapping is applied. Eccentricity, inclination and interplanetary distance ratios are preserved. Projected orbit lines can cross, including Pluto/Neptune, without a physical collision. Motion is calculated from the date rather than arbitrary animation periods. This is an educational model, not an observer's sky chart or an eclipse renderer.

Moon illumination in the Earth panel comes from geocentric Sun–Earth–Moon geometry. Season boundaries use each year's calculated equinoxes and solstices, not fixed calendar dates. Planetary guides are sampled trajectories. Satellite guides are illustrative circles at the current physical orbital radius, tilted to pass through each satellite's position; they are not full historical trajectories. The Earth panel supplies the astronomical lunar phase.

For example, Earth's mean orbital distance divided by the Sun's radius is approximately 215; the mean Earth–Moon distance divided by Earth's radius is approximately 60.3. These ratios are invariant under zoom in both views. In the model the Sun's radius is 0.6957 units, Earth's radius is about 0.006371 units and 1 AU is 149.5978707 units.

The 3D renderer subtracts a nearby origin in double precision and applies a common render-only unit conversion to **all** geometry, camera distances and clipping planes. This maintains numerical detail when approaching tiny moons without changing any physical ratios or historical positions.

Phobos, Deimos, Titan, Enceladus, Titania and Triton retain schematic circular paths, with physical mean periods and retrograde motion for Triton. Their starting phases and orbital planes are illustrative, not historical ephemerides. These moons carry an asterisk in the interface. Selecting a date gives calculated positions for the planets, Earth's Moon and Galilean moons, while these other satellite positions remain schematic.

Physical data sources:

- [JPL planetary mean radii and periods](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/phys_par.html)
- [JPL satellite mean radii](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/phys_par/)
- [JPL satellite mean orbital elements](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sats/elem/sep.html)

## 3D appearance and assets

The Sun lights textured spheres with visible day/night sides. Earth has a separate cloud layer, an atmospheric rim and night lights that fade on the day side. Earth's day, night and cloud maps are aligned to Greenwich using Astronomy Engine's [terrestrial observer vectors](https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy/blob/master/source/js/README.md#ObserverVector), including sidereal time, precession and nutation. Its geographic day/night boundary follows the selected UTC moment, including **Now**. Saturn has textured rings; Uranus has a faint illustrative ring. The smaller Martian moons have irregular shapes. Planet/Moon pole directions and spin rates use Astronomy Engine's IAU model and the shared simulation clock; texture prime-meridian alignment for bodies other than Earth remains illustrative.

Surface maps by [Solar System Scope / INOVE](https://www.solarsystemscope.com/textures/) are bundled locally under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). There is no runtime dependency on the texture host. See [asset attribution](public/textures/ATTRIBUTION.md). Pluto and satellite surfaces other than Earth's Moon are deterministic artistic approximations, not observed maps. Static cloud/surface maps do not reproduce weather or historical surface changes. Lighting exposure, atmospheric rims, irregular moon shapes and ring appearance remain illustrative; the body scales use mean physical radii. This is not a photometric or eclipse simulation.

The renderer is loaded separately from the controls. WebGL initialization/context errors offer a **2D view** fallback. GPU resources, controls, observers and animation loops are disposed when switching away from 3D.

The automated tests cover physical size/distance ratios in both views, Sun clearance across sampled orbits from 1900–2100, unchanged moon-parent distances, date validation, clock progression, relative orbital rates, lunar phases and seasons. View tests cover orbit shape/inclination, fitting every body (including tiny moons), shared scaling through zoom/resizes, pointer anchoring, camera following across date jumps, numerical rendering precision and rotation axes/rates. Earth lighting tests transform the actual textured mesh normals and compare their solar altitudes with the separate observer/horizon calculation, including Britain by day/night, other longitudes, polar regions, seasons, UK daylight-saving transition dates and years 1900–2100. Navigation tests exercise real OrbitControls with synthetic input: four-direction Space-pan, return to orbit, persistent Pan mode, right-drag/arrow keys, form focus, and shortcut cleanup after lost focus or unmount. Reference events use [NASA's lunar phase tables](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/phase/phase2001gmt.html) and [USNO's astronomical seasons](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/Earth_Seasons).
